Idea 1 of 6

Boom and bust

What people say

Invasive insects often explode for a few years and then drop.

A Penn State educator said: “We generally see a three, four, or five year build-up, then it drops off.”

What we found

NYU scientists studied almost 20,000 iNaturalist photos from 2014 to 2022. In Pennsylvania and New Jersey, numbers grew fast, then peaked and declined after four or five years.

A Pitt biologist says they breed past their food supply, then crash.

Our question for you

What causes the bust: running out of food, enemies, or something else?

Will they boom again? How many years might that take?

Sources: Owen, Meng and Winchell (2024), Popular Science (2024), The Pitt News (2024)

This idea has the most data behind it. The NYU study was led by Hannah Owen with Professor Kristin Winchell. Professor Winchell called it “a classic growth pattern that we see in invasive species as they become part of the ecological community.” She also said: “Will they swarm again like they did a couple of years ago. Probably not. But are they going away? No.” The Penn State educator is Brian Walsh. The Pitt biologist is David Outomuro. Notice that boom and bust describes the pattern, but it does not say what causes the bust. That is our question.
